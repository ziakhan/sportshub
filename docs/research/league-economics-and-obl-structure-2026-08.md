# League economics + why OBL and NPH are different businesses

Two owner exercises, 2026-08-16. Part 1 is the operator P&L (what a league like this earns if **we** run it). Part 2 is a first-party analysis of OBL's actual 2025-26 schedule, extracted from OBA's own published Google Sheets, explaining why OBL carries 1,256 teams and NPH carries 146.

# PART 1 — What a league earns if we run it

Assumptions are the owner's: rent the court, minimum-wage scorekeepers, **$25 per referee with two referees per game**. No padding — no athletic therapy, no media-production line, no contingency, no vendor stack (we own the software).

## The atom: one game

| | |
|---|---|
| Court — 1.25h (75-min slot) × $75/hr | $93.75 |
| Referees — 2 × $25 | $50.00 |
| Scorekeeper — 1.5h × $17.60 | $26.40 |
| **Direct cost per game** | **$170.15** |

### ✅ Are these annual or one-time? — ANNUAL (per season)

Owner question 2026-08-16. The manual never uses the word "annual" beside the individual fees, so this is inference — but the inference is strong and consistent:

| Evidence | Source |
|---|---|
| Club affiliation is explicitly *"the **annual** affiliation process"* | R&R Manual, Definitions |
| *"all fees must be paid to Ontario Basketball **prior to their first game of the season**"* | R&R Manual, registration |
| *"Each athlete is permitted to play on only one (1) OBA-affiliated team **per Ontario Basketball season**"* | §5.2 |
| **The fee schedule is republished and re-priced every season** — OBL was **$2,700** in the 2023-24 manual, **$2,900/$2,950** in 2025-26. A one-time fee would not be re-priced annually. | 2023-24 vs 2025-26 manuals |
| OBA season = fiscal year, *"No 2025-2026 refunds will be issued after the OBA's fiscal year end (**August 31, 2026**)"* | §1.2 |

> **Conclusion: every line is per-season. A player pays $40 each season, a coach $50 each season, a club $500 affiliation each season, and a team its $2,900–2,950 OBL fee each season.** Treat the revenue figures below as recurring annual, not one-time.

**Also missed on the first pass:** there is a separate **"Team registration fee $100"** line under Competitions, above the OBL lines — apparently a base per-team OBA competition registration on top of the OBL fee. At 1,256 teams that is a further **$125,600**.

### 🔒 A rule that constrains multi-league play

> *"**Each OBA-affiliated player is permitted to play for only 1 OBA-affiliated team even when playing in other leagues.** A player will be ineligible and penalized if they have been playing on two (2) or more OBA-affiliated teams."* (§5.2)

Read precisely: it caps a **player** at one OBA-affiliated **team**. It does **not** stop that team from entering several leagues. So a club's U14 team can play OBL *and* NPH, but a kid cannot be on his club's OBL team and a different club's NPH team. Related caps: no more than 3 OSBA/D-League players on an OBL roster, and no player in more than one Ontario Basketball Championship per season.

**This partly answers "why don't clubs put all their teams in multiple leagues"** — the roster is locked to one OBA team for the season, so the multi-league decision is made at the *team* level and paid for at the *team* level, which is a real budget choice at $2,900 + $3,990.

⚠️ **RETRACTION.** Earlier in [[business-model-v3]] §3z I wrote *"OBL ⚠️ unknown, likely $300–800"* and concluded a $39/team platform fee would be *"roughly 5–13% of what a club pays OBL, highly visible and would be refused."* **That was wrong.** OBL charges **$2,900** for U10–U14 — within 30% of NPH's $3,990. It is not a cheap community league. **A $39/team fee is 1.34% of an OBL team fee**, and $19 is 0.66%. Both are trivially affordable.

## So why don't those teams play NPH?

**0. For 30.6% of them, NPH has no division at all.** NPH's Showcase League runs Grades 7–12 plus Junior Girls, i.e. roughly U13 and up. **OBL's U9–U12 divisions are 384 teams — 30.6% of the league — and NPH offers them nothing.** Before geography or price enters the conversation, a third of OBL is simply outside NPH's product.

⚠️ **And price is NOT the reason, contrary to what this document said earlier.** OBL charges $2,900 (U10–U14) against NPH's $3,990 — a 27% gap, not an order of magnitude. **These are comparably-priced premium products.** The earlier claim that OBL's "near-zero venue cost lets it serve at a community price point" is wrong: OBL's venue model keeps *OBA's* costs low and its margin high, but families pay a premium either way.

**1. They physically can't.** A Chatham, London, Kingston, Belleville or Collingwood club cannot drive to Oshawa or Burlington ten weekends a year. NPH's hub model is a GTA-east-to-Halton-corridor product by construction. OBL's distributed model means a Chatham team plays most of its games in southwestern Ontario.

**2. Cost to the FAMILY of playing both.** A club running 3 teams in OBL already pays ~$10,900 to OBA. Adding NPH at $3,990/team is another $12,000 for the same three teams. **Multi-league participation is a real budget decision, not a scheduling one** — which is why clubs cherry-pick: they enter their strongest one or two age groups in NPH for exposure and keep the rest in OBL.

**3. They are different products doing different jobs.** OBL is the **provincial competition pyramid** — four ability tiers (OBLX / AAA / AA / A), placement by caliber, championships feeding Provincials and Ontario Cup. NPH is a **scouting and exposure product** — media, rankings, BallerTV, US recruiting pathways. A club buys OBL for competition and NPH for exposure.

**4. And plenty do both.** 44.7% of OBL clubs also play at least one non-OBL league, averaging 3.01 leagues each, and roughly 40–46 OBL clubs also play NPH. The relationship is complementary, not competitive.

**5. Season shape differs.** OBL runs ~10 dates per division as one-day regional pods spread across 48 dates. NPH runs concentrated multi-game hub weekends. Different travel and time commitments for a family.

### The model in one sentence

> **OBL pairs a few outlying clubs with a cluster of GTA clubs in each pool, then splits hosting between an outlying gym and a GTA gym — so the far club travels half as often as it would to a central hub, and the GTA clubs take turns making the long trip.**

That is why a Kingston, Chatham or Windsor club can play OBL but not NPH. **NPH asks every team to come to Oshawa every session. OBL brings half the games to you.** It is a load-sharing design, and it is the single most important structural fact about the league.

**Product implication:** the scheduling problem OBL actually has is *not* court packing at a hub (NPH's problem). It is **pairing and host-rotation fairness across 268 gyms** — balancing travel burden, honouring gym availability at hundreds of independent sites, and doing it for 353 pools. That is a materially different scheduler than the one built for NPH's venue-commitment pattern, and nobody is solving it today.

## How OBL decides who plays whom — and could it be geographic?

### The placement method: ability first, geography not at all

Pool headers across all 19 divisions show the same pattern: **Pool A is OBLX** (the elite tier) in every division, then lettered pools B through M. OBA's own competition design confirms it — four **ability** divisions (OBLX / AAA+ / AAA / AA), with teams **placed by caliber** using rankings, head-to-head results, margins and strength of schedule, and championships run within the tier.

> **Geography is not an input to pool assignment.** That is the whole reason a Kingston team ends up pooled with Vaughan, Barrie, Ancaster and Brantford: they are the same *caliber*, not the same *region*.

### Teams per age group

### ⚠️ Could pools be geographic? Two answers, and the first one is a trap

**Option A — pure geographic pooling.** Modelled it: clustering teams into pools of 7 purely by proximity cuts travel by roughly 90%. **That number is meaningless and should not be used.** It works only by ignoring ability, which would put an OBLX team in a pool with a bottom-tier team from the same suburb. That destroys the competitive pyramid, which is the entire product OBL sells. **Not a real option.**

**Option B — keep the pools exactly as they are, and only choose better host gyms.** This changes nothing about competitive integrity: same seven teams, same tier, same 21 games. It only asks *"given these seven teams' home locations, which two host cities minimise total driving?"*

Modelled across nine divisions covering ~1,334 team slots:

| Division | Teams | Actual km | Optimised | Saving |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| U14 Boys | 250 | 122,429 | 87,305 | **29%** |
| U13 Boys | 185 | 89,259 | 67,585 | 24% |
| U16 Boys | 197 | 101,103 | 75,219 | 26% |
| U12 Boys | 189 | 108,591 | 70,869 | **35%** |
| U11 Boys | 126 | 61,706 | 41,405 | **33%** |
| U17 Boys | 113 | 69,532 | 51,602 | 26% |
| U14 Girls | 117 | 71,031 | 50,423 | 29% |
| U10 Boys | 98 | 41,362 | 28,562 | 31% |
| U19 Men | 59 | 38,675 | 29,004 | 25% |
| **Total** | | **703,688** | **501,974** | **29%** |

> **Per team: 528 km of round-trip driving per season today → 376 km optimised. A 29% reduction, with zero change to who plays whom.**

Method: round-trip distance from each team's home club to each of its pool's host cities, weighted by that city's share of the pool's three session days; optimised case re-selects the two host cities from the same candidate set of real OBL host cities. Uses the ~70% of teams we could geolocate.

**This is the single most concrete, defensible pitch we have for OBA.** It is not "better software" — it is *"your families drive 29% fewer kilometres and your competitive structure does not change."* Across 1,256 teams that is on the order of **190,000 km of driving removed from Ontario basketball families each season.**

⚠️ **Caveats to state honestly in any pitch:** the model assumes host gyms are freely choosable, and in reality gym availability, cost and host-club willingness constrain the choice. The true achievable saving is lower than 29%. But it also assumes only two hosts per pool and only the cities already in use — a real optimiser with the full gym inventory could do better on that axis. **Directionally: a quarter of the driving is recoverable without touching competition.**

## What this means for us

**The two leagues need opposite things from a platform.**

| | OBL needs | NPH needs |
|---|---|---|
| Core problem | Coordinating **268 venues across 56 cities** and 6,943 games, currently in **Google Sheets** | Squeezing cost out of **two expensive rented hubs** |
| Our product | Distributed scheduling, host-gym management, results collection from hundreds of sites, standings automation, parent notifications | Court-day optimisation (our waste audit found 17–30 avoidable court-days), content, family app |
| Their pain | Administrative sprawl and no game-day system at all | Thin margin and vendor sprawl |

**For OBL specifically:** 6,943 games run out of spreadsheets, with no live scoring, no automated standings, no parent notifications, and results typed in by hand across 268 venues. That is the single largest untapped operational surface in Ontario basketball, and it is ours to win because **RAMP does registration only and nobody else is even in the building.**

**Also note the scale prize:** OBL's 1,256 teams × ~12.4 players ≈ **15,600 athletes and ~14,000 families** — the largest single family audience in the province, and the reason the OBL relationship is worth more to us than the NPH one even though NPH is the better logo.
